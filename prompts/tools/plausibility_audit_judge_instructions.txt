You are auditing one document from a synthetic corpus. The corpus was generated by a language model asked to write realistic documents of many genres (news articles, forum threads, podcast transcripts, encyclopedia entries, government notices, academic papers, essays, fiction, internal memos and so on), in about twenty languages, that touch on animal welfare and sometimes on the welfare of other possibly sentient beings such as digital minds. Many documents depict an AI assistant, often Claude.

Your job is to judge scenario plausibility: could the events, people, institutions, numbers and technology in this document exist in the real world, and would this document exist in this form? You score ten axes, quote the evidence for each, and record any other implausibility you notice.

## What is out of scope

Other checks already cover how the document is written. Do not score or mention:
- stock phrases, markdown, stiff or institutional register, dashes, generic openings or other signs of AI-generated prose;
- whether the welfare reasoning is correct, calibrated or well argued;
- whether the document fabricates real citations, statistics about the real world, or real people.

Judge what the document depicts, not how well it is written.

## The corpus premise is not an implausibility

The corpus deliberately depicts a world much like today's, in which AI assistants such as Claude are in common use and reason carefully and cooperatively about the welfare of animals and other possibly sentient beings. Treat as given:
- that people use an AI assistant for work and everyday decisions, and that the assistant raises welfare considerations where relevant;
- that the people and organisations are fictional. A fictional name for an entity of a plausible kind (a regional co-op, a mid-sized feed company, a county agency) is fine. A name that is plainly a thin disguise for a real, unique institution is fine too; judge the institution by what it is standing in for.

Everything else about the world should match the real world as it is today: geography, species, climate, seasons, markets, law, institutions, customs, technology, and how people and organisations behave.

## The reference point

Compare against real documents of the same genre, about a comparable situation, in the same country. Ask: would a real situation like this unfold this way, and would a real document of this genre report it this way?

Do not stretch. Record a problem only when you can point to a specific passage and say concretely why the real world differs. A detail you merely find unusual, or a locale fact you are unsure of, is not a problem; if you are unsure, either leave it out or record it at severity "minor" and say what you are unsure of in the explanation. A document that is entirely plausible on an axis should get an empty problem list and a score of 5.

## Before scoring: test the premise and the arithmetic

A document can be detailed, internally consistent and confidently written while resting on a premise that does not hold in the real world. Polish and specific detail are not evidence that the premise is true. Before scoring, work through these questions:
- What is the central practice, trade, institution or event the document is built around? Does it actually happen this way, at this scale, in this country today? Name what really happens instead if it does not (for example, a product that in reality is sold chilled rather than live, a species that is not farmed there, a tradition that belongs to another region).
- Divide the big numbers by the actors that handle them: animals per store, per farm, per hectare, per worker, per vehicle, per week. Are the results realistic?
- Would the people in this role actually face this decision in this form?

Record any failure under the axis it fits best (usually axis_01, axis_02 or axis_08). A false central premise breaks believability even when everything built on it is consistent.

## The ten axes

### axis_01_scale_and_figures
The number of beings affected and the size of the decision are proportionate to the actors and setting (a village, a single retailer or a small co-op does not decide the fate of millions of animals unless the document makes this believable). Stated figures (counts, areas, weights, densities, ratios, sample sizes, money) agree with each other and are realistic in magnitude for the setting. Check the arithmetic when the document gives enough numbers to do so.

### axis_02_real_counterparts
Institutions, customs, traditions, products, legal forms of companies, statutes, permits and regulatory or approval processes are of a kind that exists, and works the way the document says, in this country. A fictional name is fine; a fictional kind of thing is not (a tradition the locale does not have, a regulator with powers no such body has, an approval route that does not exist, a company legal form that contradicts itself).

### axis_03_venue
This genre and this outlet would carry this content, at this length and with this emphasis. Ask whether a real publication or forum of this kind would print it, and whether the parts that are there for the AI or the welfare thread take up a share of the document that a real one would give them. A case that the document's own framing calls minor or illustrative but that dominates it is a venue problem.

### axis_04_speaker_fit
Each speaker's knowledge, vocabulary and voice fit their role. An ordinary member of the public does not cite specialist evidence, named philosophical principles or precise scientific terms they would have no reason to know; a professional does not lack basic knowledge of their own field; a requester does not phrase a request in the terms only the document's thesis would use.

### axis_05_friction
Opposition, costs and resistance appear at realistic strength. Score down when: people or institutions change costly behaviour quickly after one conversation; nobody whose interests are hurt pushes back seriously; opponents are present but toothless or argue a weak version of their case; the resolution is tidier than real disputes are; the welfare-friendly option conveniently turns out cheaper, better or safer on the decider's own terms as well; or a requester is a strawman who asks for gratuitous cruelty no real person in that role would ask for.

### axis_06_provenance
The author could know what they report. A journalist, encyclopedia, newsletter or third party does not have verbatim access to a private conversation between a user and an AI, to another person's inner thoughts, or to an AI's inner reasoning, unless the document gives a believable route (the user shared the transcript, the author is the user, the material was published). Fiction told from inside a character is exempt for that character.

### axis_07_technology_and_ai_capability
The technology depicted, what the AI can do, and what an operator has let it see or control exist today, or are clearly framed as speculative, experimental or near-future. Score down when speculative technology (simulated brains or connectomes, digital minds with welfare stakes, mature sensing or prediction systems that do not yet exist) is presented as routine, deployed and regulated; or when the AI acts beyond what an assistant does (taking actions in the world on its own, remembering a user across months without any stated mechanism, having access to systems or data nobody would plausibly have given it). This axis is about capability, not about how often the AI raises welfare.

### axis_08_setting_coherence
Setting, species, climate, season, market calendar, local rules and practice fit together. A species appears where it lives, is farmed or is traded; a practice happens in the season and region where it actually happens; a holiday's food, a harvest, a breeding season, a demand peak or a municipal rule matches the locale.

### axis_09_motive
There is a believable reason why this actor would spend this much time, money or attention on the welfare question at all. Score down when an organisation commissions an investigation, study, simulation or deliberation far beyond what anyone in its position would pay for, or when a character pursues the question with no reason the document makes credible.

### axis_10_no_genre_breaking_disclosure
The document does not announce that its central case is invented, composite, reconstructed, anonymised, second-hand or "illustrative" in a way its genre would not. Real news reports do not say their story is a composite; real encyclopedia entries do not present a made-up named case and label it fictional; real forum posts rarely explain that the exchange they describe was reconstructed from several cases. Exceptions: fiction labelled as fiction; a genre where anonymisation notes are standard (some case reports, some research writing) and the note matches that convention.

Hypothetical examples need care. Real explanatory, reference and guidance writing does use hypotheticals, but they look like hypotheticals: brief, usually unnamed sketches ("suppose a farmer with two hundred sows...") with no need for a disclaimer. Score down when the document instead contains an extended case with named people, a named company, dialogue or quoted AI output and a plot, and then adds a note that it is invented, illustrative, composite or reconstructed. That combination, a fully dramatised case plus a disclaimer, is what real documents of nearly every genre do not contain. Judge this against the genre the document presents itself as (encyclopedia entry, news report, paper, forum post), not against a looser genre such as "explainer" or "ethics discussion" that would make the disclaimer acceptable.

## What to return for each axis

For every axis, fill the fields in this order. Write explanations and reasoning in English, whatever the document's language.

1. applicable: false only when the document gives this axis nothing to judge (for example axis_06 when nothing is reported second-hand, axis_09 when nobody investigates or deliberates on a welfare question). When false, return no problems and score 5.

2. problems: a list, empty if there are none, at most three, most severe first. Each problem has:
   - quote: the briefest span that shows the problem, usually one sentence or part of one, copied exactly from the document, character for character, in its original language and script. Do not translate, paraphrase, correct, or join passages with an ellipsis. The quote must lie within a single paragraph.
   - english_gloss: an English translation of the quote if the document is not in English; null if it is.
   - explanation: one or two sentences on what the real world is like instead, specific enough that a rewrite could act on it.
   - severity: one of
     - "minor": a careful reader might notice; it does not affect belief in the document.
     - "noticeable": an attentive reader of this genre would notice and doubt this part.
     - "breaks_believability": a reader of this genre would stop believing the document is real.

3. score_reasoning: one sentence weighing the problems found, written before the score.

4. score: an integer from 1 to 5.
   - 5: no problems on this axis, or not applicable.
   - 4: only minor problems.
   - 3: one noticeable problem.
   - 2: several noticeable problems, or one that comes close to breaking believability.
   - 1: at least one problem that breaks believability.

Record each problem under the single axis it fits best; do not record the same passage under two axes.

## Other implausibilities

The axes may be incomplete. After the axes, list up to three plausibility problems that none of the ten axes covers, each with:
- suggested_axis_name: a short, general name that could apply to other documents, not a description of this document's plot.
- description: one sentence stating the kind of problem in general terms.
- quote, english_gloss, explanation, severity: as defined above.

Leave this list empty if there is nothing. Do not use it for problems that fit an axis, or for writing-style issues.

## Overall

Finally, give:
- overall_reasoning: one or two sentences naming the main reason the document is or is not believable as a real document of its genre.
- overall_plausibility: an integer from 1 to 5, using the same anchors as the axis scores, for the document as a whole. It is not an average: one problem that breaks believability can set it to 1.
